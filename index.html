Who really drives the conversation around a brand account?

The question

U.S. embassies in Israel, Iraq and Qatar post every day to large, mixed audiences on X (formerly Twitter). A communications team needs to know which outside accounts amplify, challenge or redirect those messages, because that tells them where to listen and whom to engage.

What we did

We collected 586,736 mentions of three U.S. ambassadors' accounts over seven years using Sprinklr, then built a mention network for each country to find the accounts that sat at the center of the conversation and carried the most influence.

What we found

The study identified the influential figures at the center of each country's network: the outside accounts that shape how U.S. policy messages are discussed. The account that posts is only one voice in that conversation.

What a team should do

Map the network before planning outreach, and do it market by market. Monitor and engage the central accounts in each market instead of reusing one playbook everywhere.

What makes someone give after they said no last time?

The question

Nonprofits and brands run one campaign after another. Many people decline the first ask. Do they make up for it on the next one, and does that depend on who they are?

What we did

We ran three experiments built around a series of environmental fundraising campaigns on social media. We measured two traits, materialism and moral identity, and tested what happens when people are given a way to excuse their earlier refusal.

What we found
  • With no excuse offered, highly materialistic consumers gave more to the next campaign, whatever their moral identity.
  • Less materialistic consumers gave more only when their moral identity was high.
  • Offering an excuse shut down that make-up giving and cut donations from materialistic consumers, unless they also had high moral identity.
What a team should do

Segment follow-up appeals by values instead of sending one message to everyone who said no. Avoid follow-up copy that gives people an easy reason for their last refusal.

How professionals adopt new tools under pressure, and where the tools fail them

The question

Social media tools promised newsrooms more stories, faster. How do editors and reporters with little job security actually fold these tools into their daily work, and what breaks?

What we did

We interviewed 10 editors and reporters at popular Turkish news outlets about their routines, then coded the interviews for recurring patterns in tool use, workload and how they saw their own jobs.

What we found
  • Users described their role shifting from reporting to passing information along, "becoming aggregators rather than reporters."
  • The tools brought in more story ideas but raised the pressure to beat competitors.
  • None of the outlets had a fact-checking team or verification software, so checking social media content was the biggest bottleneck in their workflow.
  • With little job security, staff followed the platforms' logic instead of pushing back on it.
What a team should do

The unmet need is verification. A product for newsrooms wins by building fast checking into the existing workflow, not by adding more sources of content.
